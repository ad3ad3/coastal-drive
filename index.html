Scindia School, Class of 1994

The Great Coastal Drive

Mumbai to Rameswaram along India’s coastline, in the year I turned 50.

1 to 15 October 2026
Navigator and Narrator: Amit Datar

I love to travel. Work has taken me across the globe, and I’ve made the most of every chance to explore new places abroad. Yet I’ve always believed India has more to discover than anywhere else.

During my college days in Pune I had glimpsed the Western Ghats after the monsoon, lush and green, and always wanted to see more. Friends spoke of Kerala’s backwaters. Why not drive the entire stretch, I thought: down the western coast and round the southern tip of India. The idea stayed with me for years. What better year to finally do it than the one in which I turn 50?! On a break from my corporate career, in October 2026, I finally did it.

My boarding-school friend Sumant, an avid traveller himself, came along, and we drove his Jeep Compass from his doorstep in Navi Mumbai. Gaffar bhai, Sumant’s driver and our Man Friday, was a great support throughout.

We crossed creeks on car ferries, slept in sea-facing villas, a houseboat, a heritage hotel and numerous Airbnbs, visited beautiful temples, towns, forts and places of interest. Another school friend, Prashant, caught up with us towards the last stretch. The plan changed almost daily, and the trip was better for it.

This is the story, day by day, as I wrote it along the way.

For folks who may want to take up a similar pursuit, some technicals are summarized at the end that may help you plan a similar journey.

DAY 1GANPATIPULEfrom Mumbai

Thursday, 1 October

The Great Escape

Mumbai to Ganpatipule, via Agardanda, Dighi, Bagmandla, Dabhol and Tavasal

Most road trips are well planned. Ours wasn't. I flew in from Delhi and stayed at Sumant's place the night before, so we could set off early on 1 October.

I was up and ready at 6:30 am, the agreed departure time. At 7:00 Gaffar bhai arrived and began loading the car. At 7:02 Sumant announced that he would be out in 5 minutes.

For anyone who doesn't know him from our Scindia School days, Sumant was christened 'Haddi', Hindi for 'bone', owing to his rather skinny frame back then. Today he carries a considerably more substantial, bearded persona. Some things do change over the decades.

At 7:28 I was sitting with my coffee, reading a chapter on time management in The Corner Office. The irony was not lost on me. Then I discovered that Haddi had gone back to sleep behind his closed door. One kick in the backside later, the new departure time was announced: 8 am. We finally pulled out at 8:58.

Since we started from Navi Mumbai, we avoided the Thursday office traffic and were on the highway within minutes. A couple of hours in, we stopped at Joshi Wade Wale for hot vadas and chai for breakfast, then climbed through lush green ghats. It was hot and humid, but the scenery more than made up for it.

A day of ferries

We had decided to stick to the coastal route as best as possible. This involved boarding ferries, and lots of them. At Agardanda we drove the car onto the Dighi Queen and crossed the creek to Dighi. From there we drove on to Bagmandla, crossed to Vesavi, and wound through a long stretch of forest to Dabhol.

We reached Dabhol around 6 pm and rolled onto the ferry just as the sun was going down over the water. The light, the breeze and the silhouettes along the shore made for one of those moments when you forget that you have spent most of the day in a car.

By 7:50 pm we were at Tavasal, in time for what turned out to be the last ferry of the day. It was dark now. A cool breeze blew across the creek, and little lights twinkled on the far bank.

After more than 12 hours on the move, the journey I had planned for years had finally begun.

We were tired, and there was still Ganpatipule to reach. We got in at the MTDC resort at 9 pm, ate at the resort restaurant and took a short walk on the beach. Ending the first day to the sound of the Arabian Sea was deeply satisfying. Then back to the sea-facing villa, exhausted.

DAY 2GOAfrom Ganpatipule

Friday, 2 October

Quiet mornings, Goa nights

Ganpatipule to Goa

Day 2 began rather differently. At 6:30 am, remarkably, I got myself out of bed and onto the beach for an early walk and a dip. The morning was beautifully calm, with a gentle breeze, a few people in the water and waves rolling softly onto the sand. There is something peaceful about walking by the sea before the world has woken up.

After getting dressed I walked to the Ganpatipule temple, which sits right on the beach and a short walk from the resort. It was mesmerising and peaceful, one of those places where you slow down without being told to, and I spent some quiet time there before getting on with the day.

Next came an impromptu family visit, on strict instructions from my sister the night before. The car, however, decided it wasn't taking part: it wouldn't start. The MTDC staff found Gaffar bhai a mechanic, and with Haddi still asleep, I took an auto and went on my own. We had a lovely catch-up over hot coffee and homemade amba wadi at Milind kaka's beachside home. Sometimes the unplanned stops turn out to be the nicest ones.

Back at the resort, the mechanic had brought the car back to life, with a warning that the battery might need replacing somewhere along the way. We bid adieu to Ganpatipule at 11 am.

Highway or coast?

Given the delay, we chose the highway over the coastal road to make up for lost time. We realised a little late that the faster route meant giving up a couple of planned stops, including the Kunkeshwar temple. That's road-tripping. Sometimes you have to choose between exploring everything and actually getting somewhere.

Sometimes you choose between exploring everything and actually getting somewhere.

We crossed into Goa around 4 pm and stopped to meet a former colleague, Chetna, at Café Bodega, a small café tucked inside an old Portuguese house in Panaji. Chetna leads India-wide CSR at my former employer and moved to Goa a few years ago. It was great to see her in the Goa avatar! A relaxed coffee, some catching up and a pause from the road, and then we turned south.

On the drive down, we had decided to crash with a Scindian batchmate, Aditya ('Chat', as we fondly call him), who we'd learnt the day before was in Goa with a few office colleagues. We reached his resort at 9 pm. The night slowed us right down: drinks and conversation, some time in the jacuzzi, then a walk down to a beach shack for dinner.

The walk back was the perfect ending. It was pitch dark and the beach was almost invisible. All we could hear were the waves crashing onto the shore.

Looking back on Day 2A reluctant car, a compromised route and plenty of kilometres, rescued by quiet beaches, a temple, family, old friends and the sea.

DAY 3GOKARNAfrom Goa

Saturday, 3 October

Learning things the hard way in Gokarna

South Goa to Gokarna

I woke to the sound of waves. The morning made us realise we were right on the beach! After a quick coffee we went down for a morning walk and a dip in the sea. The beach was coming alive slowly: walkers and runners, a few swimmers, a lady filming reels of her yoga asanas, and fishermen coming back with their early catch. A little slice of coastal life, unfolding right in front of us.

Back at the shack everyone was up, so we sat around discussing our route and planning the next stop. We would be crossing into Karnataka on our way to Gokarna. After a heavy and rather late breakfast, we said goodbye to our friends and hit the road at 12:30 pm. Encouraged by our luck gatecrashing in Goa, we left the booking until we were on the road and reserved a room at Arthigamya Spa and Resort in Gokarna through Agoda. Big mistake! As soon as the booking was confirmed, we realised the Agoda site had quietly moved our stay to the next available date a few days later instead of the same day. I cancelled straight away and got an email saying the resort had been informed. But we were now heading into Gokarna with no room. (More on how that cancellation went in the Glovebox at the end.)

Dakshin Kashi

We reached the Shri Mahabaleshwar Temple in Gokarna at 2:30 pm, only to learn that it would not open for darshan until 5 pm. We would also need dhotis, and the queue would start at 4 pm. So we roamed the market to buy the attire and kill time, with a quick lunch of Maggi and banta (lemon soda in a marble-stoppered bottle) and a browse through some interesting spice shops, where we bought a few. By 4:30 pm we were in the queue. The wait was long, but darshan at Mahabaleshwar and the Maha Ganapati temple was divine.

Mahabaleshwar temple is an ancient Shiva shrine, traditionally dated to the 4th century, and one of Karnataka's seven Mukti Kshetras. It holds the Atmalinga of Shiva, and it is often called Dakshin Kashi, the Kashi of the South. Legend has it that Ganesha tricked Ravana into setting the Atmalinga down here, where it has stayed ever since. That is why devotees visit the Maha Ganapati temple first.

By 6:30 pm the spiritual part of the day was done. Then came the less spiritual part: finding a bed.

Now we had no booking at all, and we had completely forgotten it was a long weekend. Gokarna was packed. What followed was a two-to-three-hour hotel hunt through a town where virtually everything was full. We eventually found something decent. By then I was exhausted, so I showered, skipped dinner and went straight to bed.

Maybe booking accommodation in advance isn't such a bad idea after all. But we are on a road trip, and learning things the hard way seems to be part of the itinerary.

Looking back on Day 3Two temples, Maggi and banta, and the day's biggest lesson: on a long weekend, book ahead.

DAY 4UDUPIfrom Gokarna

Sunday, 4 October

Om, Shiva, Jatayu and two Scindians on the road

Gokarna to Udupi, via Om Beach and Murudeshwar

After the long day before, I finally slept well. Woke up at 6:45 am and walked through the quaint streets of Gokarna: small lanes, old houses, plenty of trees and a wonderfully unhurried morning feel. I found a tiny shop selling tea and onion pakoras, the kind of place you only stumble upon when you walk without a plan.

The town was waking up slowly, and you could sense that these lanes wouldn't stay quiet for long. For now, though, Gokarna was peaceful.

Thirty-six shots on a roll

Our first stop was Om Beach, named for its shape, which resembles the sacred ॐ. We walked for a while, watched the waves and had breakfast at Namaste Café, right on the beach.

Sitting there, I noticed how many people were completely absorbed in taking photographs. Children climbing the rocks for fun poses. A middle-aged man wrestling with his selfie. Two young men trying every possible angle. Two girls on their umpteenth take of an Instagram-ready shot. A newlywed couple who had hired a local photographer who took them around and advised on shots.

It made me think about how different things were when we were growing up. A roll of film gave you 24 or 36 shots, so you thought before you pressed the shutter. There was no deleting, no retakes and no checking the picture instantly. And sometimes, after waiting days for the prints, you found someone's finger covering half the frame.

Technology has changed the way we take photographs. The instinct hasn't changed. We still want to keep the moments we don't want to forget.

To be fair, Haddi and I are doing our bit on this trip to keep the camera industry alive.

A cave, a missed turn and Murudeshwar

From Om Beach we went to the Gogarbh Shiva cave, a small natural cave tucked into the rocks. Getting there feels more like finding a hidden spot than visiting a temple, and inside, a single shaft of sunlight falls through an opening in the roof.

Jatayu Teerth was less straightforward. The signboards weren't clear and, naturally, we drove straight past it. A little backtracking later, we found the path up the hill. The view was well worth the navigational adventure. It turned out to be a viewpoint, not the shrine we had assumed. We left Gokarna around 1 pm.

We reached Murudeshwar at 2:45 pm, just in time for the temple to open at 3 pm. It is famous for its colossal Shiva statue looking out over the Arabian Sea and a 20-storey gopuram that towers over the complex. The temple sits between the sea and the hills, and the setting is almost as impressive as the temple itself. We spent around an hour admiring the temple.

On the way to Udupi we made an unplanned stop at a small roadside hotel and were rewarded with some excellent dosa and chai. No research, no reviews and no recommendations: we stopped, ate and hoped for the best. The gamble paid off.

We checked into our Airbnb in Udupi at 6:30 pm. A quick shower and a little rest later, because we apparently don't understand the concept of staying in, we were off again, to Malpe Beach and dinner at Bubbles Bar.

Looking back on Day 4A few temples, a cave, a missed turn, excellent food and plenty of conversation. Four decades of friendship, and we still haven't run out of pointless things to laugh about.

DAY 5KANNURfrom Udupi

Monday, 5 October

Rain, roads and God's Own Country

Udupi to Kannur, via Kapu and Bekal

After yesterday's long day I slept properly, and for a change I was the one who got woken up in the morning. By 6:30 am we were on our way to Malpe Beach. The sky was heavy with cloud and rain seemed inevitable, but that wasn't going to stop us.

We spent almost an hour in the water, and for nearly half of it, it rained. Swimming in a warm sea while cool raindrops fell on us was quite magical. Then the rain really opened up, and we walked back to the Airbnb drenched and loving it.

I can't remember many times since my younger days when I enjoyed the rain like this. For years, my memories of rain have been traffic jams, rushing to office and worrying whether the corporate clothes would survive the journey.

No traffic. No office. No hurry. Just rain. I'm beginning to enjoy this new emotion.

Breakfast was hot upma, dosa, poori-bhaji and coffee, and it did exactly what was required.

A window for Krishna

At 9:45 am we packed up and drove to the Udupi Sri Krishna Matha, the 13th-century temple founded by the philosopher-saint Madhvacharya. What makes darshan here unique is that Krishna is seen through a small window with nine openings rather than directly. Next to it is the Kanakana Kindi, linked to the legend of Krishna turning to face his devotee Kanakadasa, who had been refused entry, and giving him darshan through the wall.

We had a mesmerising darshan, looking at those two gleaming eyes behind the window. The architecture around the sanctum was just as beautiful, intricate and calm.

From Udupi we took the Malpe–Padukere bridge and drove a particularly beautiful stretch with the ocean seemingly on both sides. We stopped at Kapu Lighthouse, where there were children flying kites, people on speedboats and a couple doing a wedding shoot. The groom wore a black suit and the bride a black gown, perfectly colour-coordinated with the black-and-white lighthouse behind them. Naturally, I couldn't resist trying the kite myself.

Into Kerala

As soon as we were in Kerala, the coconut trees took over. They were tall, really tall, each one straining to get a little higher towards the sun. Lush green everywhere. After days along the coast, it finally felt like a different landscape. God's Own Country indeed.

By 3:45 pm we were at Bekal Fort. It is one of Kerala's largest and best-preserved forts, more than three centuries old, with massive laterite walls and bastions rising straight out of the Arabian Sea. Over the years it passed from the Nayakas to Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, and finally to the British.

I walked the perimeter and took in the scenery. What caught my eye was how green everything was after the monsoon: plants everywhere, bulbuls, butterflies and even a few peacocks roaming the grounds. History on one side and the Arabian Sea on the other. Not a bad place for a pit stop.

We backtracked a little to eat at a restaurant we had passed. After a heavy, late lunch, we finally set off again at 6 pm, which of course meant more driving in the dark.

That morning, the host of the Airbnb I'd booked called to say she had accidentally double-booked. She arranged a property right across the road for the first night, and we decided to stay a second night at hers.

We reached the Airbnb for the night at 8:45 pm, a quaint beachside house built in the traditional Kerala style, with verandahs, wood and that unmistakable feeling of a home built around nature. It was the kind of place that makes you want to slow down and look around.

Looking back on Day 5Rain-soaked mornings, divine darshan, coastal roads, a fort by the Arabian Sea and, finally, Kerala.

DAY 6KANNURa day off the road

Tuesday, 6 October

Waves, walls and whisky, almost

A day in Kannur

At 5:30 am I woke to the sound of waves crashing. Gurpreet, the caretaker, was already up and making the morning chai. There is something wonderful about waking up by the sea, with no alarm and no traffic, just the waves.

You would think the sound of waves is monotonous. When you actually listen, every wave has its own sound and character. Some are louder, some softer. Some crash in with intent, others just roll in gently. Maybe I'm overthinking it. Or maybe that's what happens when you wake up next to the ocean.

When you actually listen, every wave has its own sound and character.

We walked along the beach and swam. The beach was right next to us. It was a quiet residential area so we were practically treated to a private beach! The sand was loose and not particularly soft, but the waves were strong and energetic. Back at the house I spent almost an hour on the hammock before a heavy breakfast, and then another nap. We had to wait for the previous guests to leave the original Airbnb next door, a perfectly legitimate excuse for another hour of sleep. Holiday logic.

Kannur's layers

At 12:45 pm, after checking into the original Airbnb, I went out to explore with Gaffar bhai while Haddi stayed back to relax. Our first stop was the Arakkal Museum, in the former palace of the Arakkal family, Kerala's only Muslim royal dynasty. The building caught my attention more than the exhibits: a beautiful white structure with old wooden doors and a distinctly Kerala character with a few external influences. The collection is small and some labels are only in Malayalam, but there were interesting pieces, including old telephones, a telescope, swords and a leather shield.

Next was St. Angelo Fort, built by the Portuguese in 1505 under Francisco de Almeida, their first viceroy in India. It later passed to the Dutch, the Arakkal rulers and the British, which tells you how much this stretch of coast mattered. Walking through walls that have seen centuries of traders, armies and changing rulers, the present day seems rather insignificant for a few minutes.

A few minutes away stands the red-and-white Kannur Lighthouse, close to the sea and surrounded by the green of the cantonment. Even history in Kerala comes with a generous helping of greenery.

Lunch was a lesson in working with what you've got. We made a hopeless attempt to find one particular café, gave up and landed at a multi-cuisine restaurant, where most of the menu would only be available after 6 pm! We ended up with pav bhaji and lassi.

Thoughts for the sunset

At 4 pm I was back on the beach. A sunset makes you slow down. You watch the sun slip away and the mind starts wandering: about life, the future, where we are and where we're going. Some thoughts are better left to the sunset, so I'll keep those for another time.

Haddi joined me and, naturally, we went back into the ocean for another hour of swimming, splashing and behaving like two schoolboys who had somehow been given permission to stay out late. Then home, with a few ghazals playing, a glass of rum and the sea somewhere outside. Not a bad way to end a day.

Looking back on Day 6Waves at dawn, a palace and a fort, pav bhaji by default, and a sunset that kept its thoughts to itself.

DAY 7KOCHIfrom Kannur

Wednesday, 7 October

Beaches, burgers and a divine detour

Kannur to Kochi, via Muzhappilangad, Kappad and Triprayar

A lazy morning for a change. I woke around 7 am, made a coffee and sat on the terrace above the sea with a little early sun, a gentle breeze and absolutely no reason to hurry. After a week on the road, it felt good to sit and do nothing for a while.

Driving on the beach

At 10 am, the road called. Our first stop was Muzhappilangad, one of the few beaches in India you can drive on. So, naturally, we drove on the beach. Fishermen were bringing in their morning haul of clams, which gave the slightly unreal experience an everyday rhythm.

Next was Dharmadam Beach, with Dharmadam Island sitting just offshore. At low tide you can apparently walk across. The tide was well and truly in when we arrived. So much for our island adventure.

By 1 pm, hunger had taken over. We stopped at Tabak Bun Café for some good burgers and juices, followed an hour later by the obligatory "let's get moving" feeling.

A mere 528 years late

Next was the little beach at Kappad, associated with Vasco da Gama's arrival on the Malabar Coast in May 1498, the start of a new chapter in India's relationship with the European sea powers. Historians still debate whether he stepped ashore here or at the nearby port of Panthalayani Kollam, but nobody disputes what the voyage set in motion. Mr Gama arrived in 1498. We arrived in 2026, a mere 528 years late.

Triprayar

The next stop turned out to be the highlight of the day. The Triprayar Sree Rama Temple stands on the bank of the Karuvannur river, with the deity facing the water. What caught my eye first was the outer wall lined with thousands of tiny lamp holders. They were empty when we visited, but each one holds an oil lamp on festival nights. I could only imagine how beautiful the place must look when they are all lit. Murals run around the outer walls of the sanctum, with smaller shrines to Krishna and Ayyappa on either side.

After darshan, devotees traditionally feed the fish in the river, so we did the same. Standing by the water after darshan, feeding the fish and watching the river flow, was wonderfully peaceful.

We had arrived just in time for the evening prayers, accompanied by a small ensemble on Kerala temple percussion: the chenda, the maddalam and the ilathalam. The sound, the murals, the river and the prayers all came together beautifully.

Some places you photograph and leave. Others make you wish you had stayed a little longer. Triprayar was the second kind.

By the time we came out it was dark. No more detours. We drove on to our Airbnb at Vypin, across the water from Fort Kochi, and dinner after another long day was the most sophisticated meal imaginable: Maggi!

Looking back on Day 7Beach driving, an island we couldn't reach, burgers, a 528-year-old connection and an unexpected evening of darshan by the river.

DAY 8KOCHIa day in Fort Kochi

Thursday, 8 October

Old stoves, old streets and living traditions

A day in Kochi

I woke at 6 am and made a coffee in the shared kitchen. Siju, who owns the house, was up as well and showed me around, and one thing immediately caught my eye: an enormous old wood-burning stove. It was the family's actual kitchen stove until a few years ago, when they moved to LPG. Huge, solid and nothing like a modern kitchen, it was a glimpse of how these houses cooked not long ago.

From the old kitchen I went up to the terrace for the sunrise, and finally saw a proper one. On the west coast we'd had plenty of sunsets, but sunrises had been hard to catch. From the third floor the view was clear, and there were birds everywhere, some on the terrace and many more calling from the trees.

Our own private Fort Kochi

At 8:45 am I took the ferry across to Fort Kochi, where Arun met me on his bike. He was our guide on the walking tour I booked for the day, and I got lucky: there were no other guests, so the group tour became my own private walk through the old quarter.

Our first stop was David Hall (@davidhallartgallery_cghearth), a Dutch-era building that is now an art and cultural space. There was an exhibition on, and I met Mr. Ganesh Subramaniam (@nano_shilpi), the nano artist from Kerala. His work is phenomenal. Among the pieces on display were three elephants inside the eye of a needle and a Ganesha carved on a mustard seed. Talent knows no bounds. The exhibition also had paintings by several artists, lovely to browse.

We walked on to St. Francis Church, one of the oldest European-built churches in India. The Portuguese first built it in 1503, and it has since seen Dutch and British hands. Vasco da Gama died in Kochi in 1524 and was buried here before his remains were taken home to Portugal. Standing inside, you can almost feel the layers of history.

Along the waterfront, I found myself as distracted by the wildlife as by the history: Brahminy kites overhead, birds in the trees and a lot of activity on the water. Fort Kochi has a quality where history and everyday life sit side by side without making a fuss about it.

Mattancherry and a change of plan

An auto took us to Mattancherry and the Palace Museum, better known as the Dutch Palace. The Portuguese built it in the 16th century and the Dutch later renovated it. Its murals and the artefacts of the old Cochin royal family are impressive. The palace temples were closed, so we went on to the narrow lanes, antique shops and old buildings of Jew Town.

By now it was clear that one day in Kochi wasn't going to be enough. So we changed the plan: we would stay an extra day and then do a houseboat. When we went to see one place, the owner wasn't home. By chance we bumped into his neighbour, Alex, who offered us a room he usually lets on Airbnb. We checked in and then came the most important activity of the afternoon, a one-hour nap.

Afterwards I went back to Jew Town to shop, and met Jeevan Lal (@jeevanlalmp), an artist with an amazing collection of his own work. I came away with some paintings. On the way back I found the palace temples open for darshan (what luck!), so I visited the Bhagavathy, Shiva and Krishna temples, all three of which had been closed that morning.

Kerala comes alive

Arun had arranged tickets for an evening of Kathakali and Kalaripayattu. Kathakali is Kerala's classical dance-drama, known for its elaborate make-up and costumes, expressive eyes and highly stylised movement. Its stories come from the epics, told almost entirely through facial expression and gesture. That night's performance was Kalyanasougandhikam, from the Mahabharata: Draupadi asks Bhima to fetch the fragrant Saugandhika flower, and on the way Bhima meets Hanuman, disguised as an old monkey, who humbles him.

Following the one-hour performance was a demonstration of Kalaripayattu. It is Kerala's traditional martial art and one of India's oldest, combining conditioning, weapons, flexibility and extraordinarily controlled movement.

One was all expression and storytelling, the other strength, agility and precision. Both were spectacular, in completely different ways.

We had a wonderful dinner at Rasoi. Walking back, we fell into conversation with a group of Brits who had rented a tuk-tuk and driven it all the way from Rajasthan to Kerala. We swapped road stories and football scores and said goodbye. Compared with them, our trip suddenly seemed rather comfortable.

After travelling for a week, we have finally learned one important lesson. Sometimes the best way to travel is to slow down.

Looking back on Day 8An old stove, a new sunrise, centuries of history, a private tour, serious culture and a decision to stay another day.

DAY 9ALAPPUZHAfrom Kochi

Friday, 9 October

Floating along, going nowhere

Fort Kochi to Alappuzha, and a houseboat

After a relaxed morning in Fort Kochi, we set off at 9:30 am for Alappuzha, about two hours away. The plan for the day was simple: board our houseboat and drift through Kerala's backwaters. That's exactly what we did.

The first few hours were all excitement, with photos, silly videos and messages to friends. Around us, adventurous souls in speedboats were pulling dramatic 180-degree turns to thrill their passengers. Different boats, different holiday philosophies.

After a couple of hours, with a few halts along the way, we docked near a small village just as it started to rain. Our 30-minute stop had other ideas. Some battery trouble kept us there for over an hour.

Into the backwaters

Once we were moving again, the pace of the day changed. The backwaters were serene, with narrow channels, lush green banks and glimpses of village life along the shore. We saw kites and Brahminy kites with their white heads and chests, egrets, ducks and smaller birds flitting along the banks.

We moved to the front of the boat and sat quietly with the captain, watching the scenery go by. No sightseeing checklist, no hurry and no need to fill the silence with conversation. Just the movement of the boat, the sound of the water and the birds going about their business.

Sometimes the best part of travelling is when you stop trying to do anything at all.

Around 7 pm we reached the spot where the houseboat would anchor for the night. There were a few other houseboats docked near ours. One had a group of youngsters who were dancing to music. While we could see them dance through the glass walls, we couldn't hear the music from their boat. Instead, we amused ourselves matching their moves to the music videos on our boat's TV. Some steps fit perfectly, some were hilariously off, and all of it was fun to watch.

Dinner was served and, after a day of drifting, we called it a night much earlier than usual. By 9:30 pm we were in bed. No late plans, no hunting for restaurants and no driving in the dark. Just a quiet night on the water.

Looking back on Day 9Photos, speedboats, a temperamental battery, birds and backwaters, and finally the luxury of going nowhere in particular.

DAY 10VARKALAfrom Alappuzha

Saturday, 10 October

From backwater bliss to lightning on the ocean

Alappuzha to Varkala

I woke at 6:30 am to birdsong and a sunrise worth waking up for. Our houseboat was moored somewhere quite special. On one side the backwaters were coming alive, with small boats ferrying people to work. On the other, green farmland stretched into the distance. Everyday Kerala, unfolding at its own pace.

Then I persuaded the captain to let me steer for a while, with him supervising, of course. Captain for a few minutes. Steering a houseboat is nothing like driving a speedboat. A speedboat answers the moment you turn the wheel. A houseboat takes its time. You turn the wheel a little, wait, and only much later does the bow swing round. It struck me that leading a large organisation works much the same way. The decisions that matter are small, made early, and their effect shows up long after. Leading a small team is closer to the speedboat: you turn, and you see the result right away.

A houseboat turns slowly. You make the small correction early and trust it to show up later. Leading a large organisation is no different.

Breakfast was at 8 am and by 9 am we were back on land to start our drive to Varkala. The drive to Varkala took about three and a half hours, a little slower thanks to road works on the highway. Our resort sat on the cliffs above the sea with a wonderful view. We had booked a Kerala room at the back, but its air conditioner wasn't working, so we were upgraded to a better room on the property, the Baywatch, which happened to be the only one free. It was spacious, with a private gazebo over the ocean. Once settled, we wanted to head out. The only problem: it was far too hot for the beach.

Getting walked all over

So we put the afternoon to better use at Ojaswi Wellness, for a traditional Kerala treatment called Chavitti Thirummu. Unlike a conventional massage, the therapist uses their feet, holding a rope overhead for balance, to apply controlled pressure in long, rhythmic strokes that work deep into the muscles. We discovered it was actually a hospital that also treats in-patients for spinal injuries, and the therapists were Kalaripayattu practitioners themselves. So we put ourselves in their hands. Or rather, their feet.

And boy, did it work. I can't describe the knots it opened up. Afterwards I felt as though someone had taken my body apart, serviced the moving parts and put everything back together. I'd highly recommend it to anyone carrying a few decades of accumulated knots.

We ate at a café on the south cliff, and around 4:30 pm, with the sun starting to dip, went down to the beach near our resort. The waves here were much stronger than anywhere we had been, and what struck us was the force of the water rushing back after each wave broke. Beautiful, and definitely not to be taken lightly. We had to be careful not to get pulled out to sea.

Nearby, a group of young couples had set up an elaborate floral backdrop for their reels. They were clearly having a great time creating their perfect beach moments. Sumant and I went back to the pool for a swim, and then it started to rain. I couldn't help wondering how their shoot was going. Nature clearly hadn't checked their content calendar.

When the ocean lit up

Given the weather, we asked the chef for pakoras and chai. Cool wind, waves crashing below, hot pakoras and tea. Sometimes the simplest pleasures are the best.

Around 9 pm, as we ordered dinner (the hotel crew couldn't have been more helpful), nature began a show of its own. As the night went on, I watched one of the fiercest displays of lightning I have experienced. The sky would blaze and light up the whole ocean in extraordinary detail for a few seconds, and then everything went pitch dark again. Another flash. And another. We spent hours watching. I managed a few shots, but no photo or video could capture the scale of it. It was one of the most memorable nights of my life.

That morning we had watched the sun rise over peaceful backwaters. That night we watched lightning set the Arabian Sea ablaze.

DAY 11KOVALAMfrom Varkala

Sunday, 11 October

Kanniyakumari, almost

Varkala to Kovalam, via Jatayu Earth's Center

The sea was calm again in the morning, after the most furious lightning I have ever seen. At a time when nations are busy flexing their military might and fighting wars for supremacy, nature offers a humbling reminder of how insignificant our power really is. A flash of lightning or a raging sea can put us in our place in an instant. Perhaps we should be a little more humble before nature decides to remind us.

We spend so much of our lives trying to control things and proving our own importance. Then nature puts on a display like this, with no effort and no announcement, and reminds us who is really in charge.

Jatayu's last stand

We bid goodbye to the lovely staff at the resort and reached Jatayu Earth's Center at Chadayamangalam, in Kollam district, by 10:30 am and took the cable car to the top of the rock. The sculpture, by the artist and filmmaker Rajiv Anchal, is about 200 feet long, 150 feet wide and 70 feet high, and it is said to be the largest bird sculpture in the world.

According to the Ramayana, it was here that Jatayu fell. The great bird had tried to stop Ravana from carrying Sita away, knowing full well how strong the demon king was. Ravana cut his wings, and Jatayu crashed onto these rocks, living just long enough to tell Rama which way Sita had been taken.

It takes courage to stand up for what is right, especially when you know how strong the other side is.

We also had darshan at the Ram temple next to the sculpture. The idols of Lord Ram, a large one behind and a smaller one in front, were beautiful. We must have been doing something right, because we got in just before the temple closed for the afternoon.

A change of plan

Then the cable car down, and on to Kanniyakumari. Well, not quite. We had been posting about our adventures on our Scindia alumni chat group as our classmates were following our journey. Another friend called to say he wanted to join us for the remaining stretch, so after some quick re-planning we decided to stay in Thiruvananthapuram and pick him up at the airport the next day.

After an hour of searching we booked a room at the Pappukutty resort on Kovalam beach. The beach is short but the waves are strong, and the lifeguards were making sure nobody went too deep. A quick dip in the sea, then the pool, then a walk along the shore to the cafés for dinner. Every few seconds, the beam from the lighthouse swept out over the dark water, a guide for the ships out at sea.

DAY 12KANNIYAKUMARIfrom Kovalam

Monday, 12 October

Divine darshan, ocean melodies and the return of Laddu

Kovalam to Kanniyakumari, via Thiruvananthapuram

The day began early, with a 6 am start for a date with the divine. We arranged an auto, left the car parked near the beach and set off for two of Kerala's most remarkable temples.

First was the Aazhimala Shiva Temple, high above the sea, where we had darshan of Mahadev. The highlight was the 58-foot statue of Shiva as Gangadhareshwara, holding Ma Ganga in his matted locks as she descends to earth. It was still partly wrapped in scaffolding when we visited - probably being painted ahead of some celebrations. Below it, a cave-like gallery tells stories of Shiva in sculpture. Quite a sight, and a lovely way to begin the day.

Next came the much-awaited Sree Padmanabhaswamy Temple, where Vishnu reclines on the serpent Anantha. The enormous reclining idol is seen through three separate doors: the head and upper body through the first, the navel with Brahma seated on a lotus through the second, and the feet through the third. It was a fascinating experience, though I found myself wishing I could see the whole idol at once.

Back at the hotel we had brunch and a well-earned nap. Spirituality is wonderful, but it can be a tiring business.

The nightingale by the sea

By 4 pm we were packed and on our way, with a stop at an old coffee house near the airport overlooking the ocean. As we sipped our coffee, lightning flickered over the sea in the distance, with the occasional rumble of thunder. Nature seemed determined to provide the entertainment.

Then came an unexpected little moment. Walking back to the car, I heard music over a loudspeaker and followed it to a woman singing live, beautifully. Her name was Soumya, and she comes to the beach regularly to sing. We stopped to listen, she graciously took a request, and for a while there was nowhere else we needed to be.

Sometimes the best experiences are the ones you never planned for.

Then it was time for the main event of the evening so we headed to the airport to pick up our third musketeer - Prashant (christened ‘Laddu’ at school, after the round Indian sweet, for reasons he would rather we didn’t explain). With Haddi and Laddu, the gang was at full strength, and the banter was immediately restored to dangerous levels.

We drove on to Kanniyakumari in the dark, had a quick dinner and arrived at our Airbnb around 10:30 pm. That was followed by an hour or two of the usual meaningless banter, the kind that makes perfect sense to old friends and none at all to anyone listening. And then, finally, sleep.

DAY 13RAMESWARAMfrom Kanniyakumari

Tuesday, 13 October

Land's end, three seas and the 22 wells

Kanniyakumari to Rameswaram, via Pamban

After 12 days on the road, we were finally at Kanniyakumari, the southern tip of India.

The morning started with a crisis. The Airbnb had no tea or coffee, and since we had arrived very late the night before, nobody had checked. Laddu was very frustrated. The next hour was spent calming him down, until we walked to a tea shop nearby and had a fresh brew made for him. Peace was restored.

Where three seas meet

From the shore we looked out at the meeting of the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the Indian Ocean, and took the ferry out towards the two monuments on the rocks offshore. Swami Vivekananda swam out to this rock towards the end of 1892 and meditated there for three days and nights. The memorial on the rock was opened in 1970. Beside it stands the 133-foot statue of the Tamil saint-poet Thiruvalluvar, completed on 1 January 2000. Its height matches the 133 chapters of his Thirukkural. Since December 2024, a 77-metre glass bridge has linked the two rocks. As the boat neared the dock at the memorial, we were surprised by the long queue of people waiting to return to the mainland. Given our plans to drive on to Rameswaram, we decided against getting off and returned on the same boat. We had to avoid the coastal route and drive inland to Thoothukudi due to some rain advisories in the region.

After a quick breakfast at a local shop, we set off for Rameswaram.

Over the Pamban bridge

Rameswaram sits on Pamban Island, and the road onto it runs right alongside the railway bridge across the sea. The old Pamban bridge carried trains from 1914 until 2022. The new one, India's first vertical-lift sea bridge, opened in April 2025, and as we made a quick stop to take photos, a train came rolling across it.

We checked into a hotel and went straight to the Ramanathaswamy Temple. Tradition holds that Rama worshipped Shiva here after the war with Ravana, and the temple is one of the twelve Jyotirlingas. Its third corridor, lined with more than a thousand pillars, is said to be the longest temple corridor in the world.

The pilgrimage follows an order. We first took a dip in the sea at Agni Theertham. Then we went through the 22 theerthams, the holy wells inside the temple, being doused with water from each one in turn. Only then did we join the line for darshan.

Sea, then 22 wells, then the lord. Some things are meant to be done in order.

Phones weren’t allowed inside the temple, so we had left ours at the hotel. That turned out to matter. Haddi assured us the hotel was a five-to-ten-minute walk away and confidently pointed the way. Twenty minutes later, we were hopelessly lost in the lanes of Rameswaram. I swore never again to trust Haddi’s sense of direction and flagged down an auto. The driver, a local, promptly typed the hotel’s name into Google Maps, and we returned to our hotel to freshen up.

Near the hotel was a small restaurant advertising a multi-cuisine menu. There was nothing else around, so despite Laddu’s reluctance, we went in. We ordered a mix of Rajasthani and Maharashtrian dishes, and the food was amazing. Every dish was excellent, and we ate to our hearts’ content. It was one of the best meals we’d had in days. Afterwards I went into the kitchen to compliment the chefs. One was from Tripura, the other from Rajasthan. Here we were at the southern tip of India, eating food from the west, cooked by chefs from the north-east and west. Diversity of talent really does work wonders.

Food from the west, cooked by chefs from the north-east and west, served at the southern tip of India. Diversity of talent works wonders.

Haddi, Laddu and I talked for hours, going back over the trip so far. They wouldn't let me go to sleep before midnight, when they wished me a happy birthday.

DAY 14MADURAIfrom Rameswaram

Wednesday, 14 October

Fifty, and content

Rameswaram to Madurai, via Dhanushkodi

I am 50!

It was raining in the morning, so perhaps fewer people had turned up at the temple. We realised that the temple was only a five-minute walk from our hotel, if only we'd taken the right lane. We had a laugh remembering our ordeal the previous evening.

Laddu and I walked over, offered our prayers and an abhishek, and had a beautiful, unhurried darshan. The temple was calm and quiet. Rameswaram was always on the itinerary, but by the original plan we would have come and gone a day or two before my birthday. The detours and late starts had quietly rearranged the calendar, and brought me here for the morning I turned 50. It didn’t feel like a coincidence. I came away feeling truly blessed.

We packed, stopped for breakfast and drove to Dhanushkodi, at the very end of Pamban Island. It was a busy town once, with a railway station and a ferry to Ceylon, until a cyclone struck on the night of 22–23 December 1964. A train full of passengers was lost, along with the town itself, and it has been called a ghost town ever since. Today a new road runs all the way to Arichal Munai, the tip, about 24 kilometres from Sri Lanka. It's also where tradition places the start of Ram Setu, the bridge Rama's army built to Lanka.

Driving that road, with the sea on both sides, was great fun. Plenty of other people had come to see it too, all of us clicking pictures and enjoying it.

Content

As we drove out of Rameswaram, Haddi asked me how it felt to turn 50.

"Content," I said.

I meant it. I was happy and grateful to be doing this trip just as I had wished for so many years, with friends I have known since boarding school. And things had kept falling into place, day after day: an unplanned stop, a friend who happened to be in town, a temple that opened just as we arrived.

Content. Blessed to be doing this trip just as I had wished, and grateful that things kept falling into place.

A change of plan, and a party

We drove on to Madurai, a change of plan after much discussion over the last two days. Laddu had to fly back to Delhi. Apparently what he could do on a laptop and phone in an air-conditioned room in Gurgaon, he could not do from a similar room in Madurai or Puducherry. Haddi also had to get to Bangalore to see his daughter on the evening of the 15th. So Madurai it was.

We checked into Heritage Madurai, a beautiful property in the heart of the city, built around a 300-year-old banyan tree and full of interesting plants. The cottages were lovely. We booked into the Skywatch Bungalow which had an expansive window looking onto the greens and 15-foot-high wooden roof with shifting panels that gave way to the glass ceiling for an excellent sky view.

Ciao

A last morning in Madurai

Content, but already itching to get back on the road. Sooner rather than later.

2. The Journey

  • We started driving from Navi Mumbai and crossed Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala and into Tamil Nadu.
  • Planned for 4-6 hours of driving a day and avoided driving after dark. Some coastal stretches have smaller towns that close early, and you don't want car trouble on a secluded road at night.
  • Highways: We mostly followed NH-66, the highway that runs down the west coast. It was good through Maharashtra. The state highways closer to the coast were narrow but in good condition.
    • The highway is being widened in Kerala, so expect diversions and single-lane stretches through construction. On some of them we averaged 40 km/h.
  • Fuel: Diesel went from about INR 98–99 a litre in Maharashtra to INR 104-105 as we moved south into Kerala.
  • Tolls: We had estimated INR 6,000–7,000 in tolls for the trip. Instead, we bought the FASTag annual pass on the Rajmargyatra app for INR 3,000, which covers 200 toll crossings on national highways over a year. It got us through almost all the tolls on this trip.
  • Ferries: Our ferry crossings on the Konkan coast took about 20–30 minutes each and cost around INR 200–300 with the car.
    • We crossed:
      • Agardanda–Dighi
      • Bagmandla–Vesavi
      • Dabhol–Dhopave
      • Tavasal–Jaigad
    • The ferries run to a fixed schedule, usually every 30–45 minutes, so check the timings ahead and expect some wait when you arrive. There is no advance booking.
    • Schedules also depend on the weather. Around the monsoon, rain and wind can disrupt them, so check the forecast the day before.
    • The ferries are an experience in themselves, and a rest while you are still moving. The route is usually shorter than driving round, though that depends on the wait at each crossing.
  • Google Maps was spot on most of the time, but zoom out now and then to check the bigger picture. Occasionally it sends you round a loop even when a more direct road exists.

3. The car

  • The Jeep Compass was a comfortable drive. I'd recommend an SUV for a trip like this, though a sedan or hatchback is fine if you stick to the highways.
  • Get the car serviced and check tyres and fuel before you set off. Jump leads, a tyre inflator and a first-aid kit can save you time hunting for a mechanic in an emergency.
  • We had no trouble finding Indian Oil fuel stations along the way.
  • With a driver, we didn't have to worry about parking. If you are driving yourself, allow extra time for parking and walking.
  • In towns, consider hiring a local auto-rickshaw: the drivers know the routes and you avoid the parking hassle. Most temples don't allow phones, so agree on a meeting point with the driver before you go in.

4. Our Stays

  • Airbnbs worked better for us. They had room for an extra person (our driver), while some hotels and resorts had no driver's accommodation at all. They also come with a kitchen and a washing machine, which are very handy on a trip like this.
  • Airbnbs also offer boutique stays and experiences a hotel may not. Dining options can be limited, though the caretakers usually cook a decent breakfast.
  • As we drove south, fewer Airbnbs included breakfast. That didn't bother us, as we wanted to try the local food and most stays were just for the night.
  • Check that your room has a way to make tea or coffee. We learnt this one in Kanniyakumari.

5. Food and water

  • Always carry water and snacks. We carried steel bottles and refilled them every morning before setting off.
  • Vegetarian and non-vegetarian food was easy to find throughout.
  • At roadside places, check that the kitchen looks clean, and give them a miss if you don't have a strong stomach.

6. Temples: timings and dress codes

  • Most temples in the south have a strict dress code for men: a dhoti, with an angavastram or bare-chested. You can drape the dhoti over shorts. After Gokarna, we kept a dhoti and angavastram in the car for quick changes. If you forget, they are usually sold outside the temples for INR 200–400.
  • Most temples close in the afternoon, typically 11 am–2 pm or 12–3 pm, so check before you plan. Local festivals can change the schedule too. We reached the Sree Padmanabhaswamy temple at 7 am, as per the timings on its website, only to find that morning darshan had closed at 6:30 and would reopen at 11 because of a local festival. The special timings had been published only in the local language.
  • Phones are not allowed in most temples. Some, like Sree Padmanabhaswamy, also restrict smartwatches, and even checked for Meta glasses.
  • Darshan queues usually start forming one to two hours before opening. Most temples sell VIP passes, though we rarely needed one. At Sree Padmanabhaswamy, the VIP pass gets you into the queue closest to the idol; it isn't necessarily faster.
  • Early mornings and evenings are usually the best times to visit.

7. Timing and booking

  • October was hot and humid on the coast, especially between 11 am and 3 pm, though mornings and evenings were pleasant. We lived in shorts and T-shirts and never wore jeans or trousers. Just remember the temple dress code above.
  • Carry an umbrella. It helps in the sun as well as the rain.
  • Watch out for long weekends and festivals. If you travel then, book ahead, especially at tourist hotspots.

A cautionary tale: booking through Agoda

We booked a room at Arthigamya Spa and Resort, Gokarna, through Agoda. Once the booking was confirmed, we saw that the site had changed our dates to the next available date rather than the one we had searched for. I raised a cancellation with Agoda immediately and received an email confirmation, with a reference number, saying the resort had been informed. I also called Arthigamya, who asked me to take it up with Agoda.

Getting a response from Agoda over the next seven to eight days was a painful exercise. When I finally got through on one of the numbers listed on their website, the person asked me to move the call to WhatsApp, then to install a file and share my screen. When I asked why he needed my screen to cancel a booking at their end, he hung up.

Eventually I wrote to Arthigamya directly. They confirmed by email that Agoda had never contacted them about the cancellation, despite the confirmation and reference number Agoda had sent us. So the resort held the room for the next day, and when we didn't turn up, it counted as a no-show. We never got a refund.

What we'd tell anyone booking online:

  • Check the dates on the final confirmation page before paying, not just on the search screen.
  • Screenshot the booking and any cancellation confirmation, including the reference number.
  • When you cancel through a booking site, confirm directly with the property, in writing, that they have received it.
  • Never install an app or share your screen with anyone claiming to be customer support. A genuine booking site never needs it.

8. Experiences worth planning around

  • Fort Kochi: book a walking tour. Having someone explain the history as you explore makes all the difference. I did the Fort Kochi walking tour with Arjun on Airbnb, and it was well worth it. Arjun also helped us book the houseboat and tickets for the Kathakali and Kalaripayattu shows at short notice.
  • Alappuzha: stay overnight on the houseboat rather than doing a day cruise. Ours had mostly vegetarian options for breakfast and dinner.
  • Kanniyakumari: ferries to the Vivekananda Rock Memorial run through the day, and the schedule is online. A VIP ticket lets you skip the queue on the way out, but the return is first come, first served.
  • Pamban bridge: you cross it on the way to Rameswaram. It's a two-lane road, so stop only briefly, and only when there is no traffic.
  • Dhanushkodi: I've been twice, both times in the morning. Try to be there by 8 or 9 am, before it heats up. Evenings are pleasant too, but the point closes around 5 pm.

9. Budget

We had estimated about INR 1.5 lakh for the trip, covering fuel, tolls, food, stays, entry fees and parking, including our driver's food and stay. We came in within budget. Roughly:

  • Fuel: INR 20,000–25,000 (diesel; depends on your car's mileage)
  • Tolls: about INR 6,000 (the annual pass brought this down to INR 3,000)
  • Food: INR 3,000–4,000 a day
  • Stays: about INR 5,000 a day
  • Parking, entry fees and the like: about INR 1,000 a day

We overspent at hotels where we had to book a separate room for our driver, but made it up on the Airbnbs.

10. What we'd do differently

  • Leave on time, especially when you are moving every day. You fit in more along the way and avoid driving in the dark.
  • Allow extra time for places that are a little off the route.
  • Travel light. You'll be shopping along the way.
  • Keep a slower day every couple of days to unwind from back-to-back travel.
  • Avoid sightseeing around midday, when it gets very hot and humid.
